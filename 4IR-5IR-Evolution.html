Is South Africa Ready to Capitalise on the 4IR and 5IR?

South Africa is well-positioned to benefit from the Fourth and Fifth Industrial Revolutions, but that position remains brittle unless technological adoption is matched by digital inclusion, reliable infrastructure, skills development and human-centred design.

Aphelele CeleISTN731Honours in Information Systems & TechnologyUniversity of KwaZulu-Natal2026
Position

Opportunity exists — but readiness remains fragile

Given, now that South Africa leads the continent in AI adoption, hosts most of Africa's data infrastructure, and has a national commission and a draft AI strategy to address the transition, it is well-positioned to benefit from the Fourth and Fifth Industrial Revolutions (Hiebert, 2025).

But rather than being secure, that stance is brittle. South Africa runs the risk of creating the exact “house of cards” that the World Economic Forum warned about, a nation that appears technologically advanced on the outside but whose institutional and infrastructural underpinnings are still unable to support it, if intentional, concurrent investments are not made in digital access, energy reliability, skills, and inclusive design (World Economic Forum, 2016).

My position is therefore that the 4IR and 5IR are worth pursuing, but only alongside, not after, the structural work needed to make their benefits broadly reachable.

Counterargument

Could accelerated adoption deepen inequality?

The strongest opposing view is that the 4IR/5IR narrative overstates South Africa's readiness, and that pursuing it now risks deepening, not closing, existing inequality.

Dube and Mahhumane (2025) argue that while 4IR technologies are intended to improve workplace practices, many South Africans remain disadvantaged by the digital divide and cannot fully benefit from the opportunities 4IR presents, while labour-rights frameworks have not kept pace with what these technologies now do.

Costa et al. (2022) extend this critique globally by questioning whether the 5IR's promise of harmonious human-machine collaboration is realistically available to civil society at large, or whether it mainly describes economies and firms already wealthy enough to invest in both the technology and the human-centred design layered on top of it.

On this view, South Africa's leading AI-adoption rate is not automatically proof of readiness. It may simply mean the country is furthest ahead in adopting technologies whose costs and benefits are unevenly distributed. The data-centre boom's electricity and water demands are one example of a cost concentrated locally while much of the commercial benefit may accrue elsewhere.

This objection is serious, and it is largely correct as a description of the risk. Where I disagree is with the implied conclusion that South Africa should therefore slow or avoid 4IR/5IR adoption. The mobile-money and WaterCopilot examples show that leapfrogging can genuinely reach underserved populations when it is designed around their specific constraints rather than imported wholesale.

The problem identified by the counterargument is real, but it is also a design and policy failure, not an inherent property of the technology. This is why my position is conditional rather than unconditional: the same evidence supporting the sceptical view also points to what has to change for the optimistic case to hold.

South Africa's Current Position

South Africa is not approaching 4IR and 5IR from a standing start

ITWeb (2026) reports that 23.1% of the country's working-age population used an AI product in the first quarter of 2026, the strongest adoption rate on the continent. This sits alongside real policy infrastructure: the Presidential Commission on the Fourth Industrial Revolution, established in 2019 and gazetted in 2020, set out recommendations spanning AI institutions, advanced manufacturing and digital infrastructure, with an explicit goal of a globally competitive, inclusive and shared economy. South Africa's Draft National AI Policy, published for comment in April 2026, extends this into AI-specific governance.

Leapfrogging as an opportunity

The clearest opportunity is leapfrogging: skipping infrastructure stages other economies had to build sequentially. Sub-Saharan Africa already leads the world in mobile money adoption, with more than a billion accounts and over a trillion US dollars in annual transactions moving entirely outside traditional banking (Kouassi Yeboua, 2026).

The Limpopo Digital Twin and its WaterCopilot AI assistant show what applied leapfrogging looks like in practice. Water managers across South Africa, Botswana, Mozambique and Zimbabwe can query complex hydrological data in plain language without needing specialist technical skills (International Water Management Institute, 2026).

Manufacturing data also reinforces the case for cautious optimism: Habanabakize (2024) found ICT adoption, innovation, investment and education levels significantly and positively associated with employment in South African manufacturing over the period 1990–2023.

Challenges

Challenges South Africa must overcome

Challenge 01

Digital divide

The divide goes beyond devices and connectivity. Many South Africans remain unable to benefit fully from 4IR opportunities, and language exclusion creates an additional barrier where AI systems do not support the languages people use for healthcare, banking and other essential services.

Challenge 02

Infrastructure capacity

South Africa's data-centre sector is expanding, but data centres are electricity- and water-intensive. Rapid AI infrastructure expansion without reliable energy planning creates a genuine structural risk.

Challenge 03

Unequal distribution

Even where 4IR/5IR technologies succeed, their benefits are not automatically shared. New careers in green energy, software development and data science often require tertiary education and digital literacy that remain unevenly distributed.

Challenge 04

Global AI risks

Adverse outcomes of AI, misinformation, disinformation and geoeconomic confrontation create risks South Africa cannot avoid simply because it is technologically advanced. Greater integration into global AI infrastructure can increase exposure.

Recommendations

Changes necessary to capitalise on the opportunity

1. Close the access layer of the digital divide

Continued investment in affordable connectivity and devices must be paired with locally relevant, language-inclusive technology design from the outset rather than importing global tools and hoping they translate. The WaterCopilot and AI-Farmer examples show what locally designed systems can look like.

2. Treat energy and digital infrastructure as one planning problem

Approving new data-centre and AI infrastructure without a credible, funded plan for the electricity and water demand it creates risks fragile, appearance-over-substance growth.

3. Update labour protection and skills pipelines

Labour and education policy need to keep pace with technological change. Entry-level roles exposed to automation should be replaced by genuine pathways into the new higher-skilled roles 4IR/5IR industries create, rather than simply disappearing.

4. Build toward 5IR human-machine collaboration

South Africa's approach should explicitly build toward harmonious human-machine collaboration rather than treating automation as an end in itself. Inclusion has to be part of the design brief from the beginning.

Conclusion

Ready to advance — but not ready to become complacent

South Africa's 4IR/5IR position in 2026 is a genuine asset, not a starting deficit: leading adoption, functioning policy infrastructure and real leapfrogging examples already exist.

But none of this guarantees the benefits reach the people who need them most, and the same country leading African AI adoption also carries severe exposure to its risks.

Capitalising on the 4IR and 5IR responsibly means treating digital inclusion, energy planning and labour protection as prerequisites running alongside technological adoption, not problems to be solved once growth has already happened.
